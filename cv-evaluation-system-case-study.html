Executive Summary

The product had to do more than recognize that a motion occurred. It had to determine who and what to track, when meaningful events occurred, which frames contained the correct biomechanical state, what to measure from those frames, and how to convert those measurements into a useful evaluation.

That created a cascading quality problem. A training-data ambiguity could weaken a model. A late event boundary could select the wrong frame. A mathematically valid formula could still measure the wrong physical behavior. A scoring rule could amplify an incorrect measurement into misleading feedback.

I helped lead a connected improvement program across those interfaces — redesigning annotation requirements around new model inputs, defining more deterministic temporal boundaries, auditing implemented measurement formulas against their intended coaching purpose, refining metric timing and configuration, and establishing multiple QA paths to isolate where errors originated.

The result was not simply a more accurate detector. It was a more explicit and testable evaluation system.

Key Product Decisions

Treat accuracy as a systems problem

Why

The first instinct in an ML product can be to assume a model needs retraining. Evaluation quality depended on a chain of data, model, timing, geometry, configuration, and scoring decisions.

Tradeoff

Slower than a single-point fix — required working backward from incorrect product behavior across every layer rather than forward from one favorite technical solution.

What it enabled

A repeatable way to locate which layer actually caused a bad output, instead of guessing.

Maximize information gain, not annotation volume

Why

Not every plausible new label was worth its annotation cost. New datapoints were added only when they had a clear downstream use.

Tradeoff

Deliberately left some theoretically useful signals unlabeled at scale, favoring targeted proof-of-concept subsets over exhaustive coverage.

What it enabled

A training-data revision scoped to what the model and downstream evaluation actually needed.

Prefer reliable inputs over richer-but-fragile ones

Why

Several theoretically attractive inputs were operationally fragile under occlusion, tracking uncertainty, or inconsistent availability.

Tradeoff

Sometimes gave up geometric precision on paper for a simpler measurement that behaved consistently in real footage.

What it enabled

Metrics that stayed faithful to their intended meaning across a wider range of real conditions.

Separate detector correctness from metric correctness

Why

When upstream detection timing was under investigation, waiting for that fix would have blocked all downstream evaluation work.

Tradeoff

Required building a controlled-input testing path rather than only testing end to end.

What it enabled

Metric validation and detector debugging to proceed in parallel instead of blocking on one another.

05

Outcome

Across the broader improvement initiative, measured model accuracy moved from approximately 70% to approximately 90%.

Broader improvement initiative. Training-data, temporal logic, model, measurement, and QA changes all contributed.

The training-data redesign was a significant contributor, but the program also included changes to temporal event logic, measurement definitions, model implementation, heuristic timing, and QA. The value of the work was larger than a single accuracy metric — it produced a system that was easier to reason about:

  • Training signals had clearer purpose
  • Temporal events had explicit detection criteria
  • Measurements were tied to intended physical behaviors
  • Metric timing became configurable and testable
  • QA could isolate failures by layer
  • Technical documentation preserved the intended meaning of the system for future contributors
